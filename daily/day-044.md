# Day 044: The promise that expires when you need it

**Tue 2026-08-04 · Govern · Day 44 of 49**

Thirty-one files here end by calling themselves living documents, and twenty-two of those name what
their next pass will do. Every one of those lines was written by something that shipped every day, and
the daily cadence was the mechanism behind them. In five days that mechanism stops, leaving a reader
who opens any of those files holding a commitment with nobody scheduled against it.

## Shipped

[governance/maintenance-policy.md](../governance/maintenance-policy.md), which settles what this repo
owes once the daily entries stop. It commits to three things and refuses everything else: the check
stays running and a red result gets fixed, a factual error gets corrected and bumps the file's version,
and a dead external link gets repaired. A better framing, a sharper argument, or a fifth metric are
named as non-triggers, because those are the ordinary reasons a project never actually ends.

On the living-document footers it does not edit anything. Rewriting twenty-two files on the way out
would be the kind of retcon this repo has refused for six weeks, so one rule declares how to read all of
them instead: a next-pass line is a named piece of open work with nobody assigned, which is what it
always literally said. It stays true and stops implying a date it never wrote down.

It also answers whether a repo carrying four known unfixed defects can call itself complete, with the
rule that a known defect may stay unfixed and may not stay unwritten, plus two bounds so that is not a
license: anything that would mislead a reader following the file, or make the thing unsafe to run as
documented, gets marked unfit rather than disclosed.

Alongside it, [.github/workflows/checks.yml](../.github/workflows/checks.yml) gained a weekly schedule
and a manual trigger, and [the check's notes](../checks/README.md) went to v3 to record why.

## What I learned

The policy's first obligation was going to be "the CI stays on, and a red check gets fixed." Checking
that sentence before shipping it turned up that it was false. The workflow fired on push and pull
request and nothing else. A finished repo does not push. So the single obligation in the whole policy
that does not depend on a person remembering to care would have stopped firing on exactly the day the
policy took effect, and every week after that, for as long as the repo stayed finished.

Push-only enforcement watches a repo under active development. It expires the moment the repo stops
being actively developed, which is the moment the watching becomes the entire point. The check had been
running green for a week and that told me nothing, because it was running for a reason that was about to
disappear.

The fix was four lines of YAML, which is the same trade this repo made once before when it chose to
ship enforcement rather than write a rule asking for it. The general shape is worth keeping: a control
tied to an activity dies with the activity. Before trusting one, ask what has to keep happening for it
to fire, and whether that thing is still going to be happening when you need the answer.

## Next

The policy assumes somebody could pick this up, and every guide here is written for a reader already
standing inside the loop. Nothing yet says what a team should take first, what to rename, or which parts
break in the first two weeks of real use. That is the difference between a system that is finished and
one that is portable.
